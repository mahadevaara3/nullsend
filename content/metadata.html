What your photos say about you

A photo file holds two things: the pixels you see, and metadata you don't. Phones and cameras write the metadata automatically, and most people never look at it. Here is what it commonly contains:

  • Exact GPS coordinates. Phones with location enabled record where each photo was taken, often to within a few meters. A photo of your living room can carry your home address.
  • Date, time and time zone. The moment the shutter fired, not when you shared it.
  • Device make, model and serial numbers. Many cameras record a body serial number, and some record a lens serial number too. The same serial in two photos links them to the same device, even when posted under different names.
  • Software and edit history. Which apps touched the image, and sometimes the steps taken in them (stored as XMP).
  • An embedded thumbnail. A small preview copy inside the file. Some editors crop or retouch the main image but leave the original thumbnail untouched, so the "removed" part is still in the file.
  • Extra images after the main one. Modern phone photos can contain HDR gain maps, depth maps or other images appended after the end of the main picture.
  • Author and place names. IPTC and XMP fields for photographer name, copyright, city, state and country.

AI images carry their own fingerprints

Images made or edited with AI tools often record that fact, and sometimes much more:

  • C2PA Content Credentials. A cryptographically signed record of how an image was made and edited. Adobe, OpenAI, Google and several camera makers write it. Metadata X-ray shows the tool that signed it.
  • IPTC Digital Source Type. A standard field that marks an image as trainedAlgorithmicMedia (generated by AI) or compositeWithTrainedAlgorithmicMedia (edited with AI).
  • Your full prompt. Stable Diffusion front-ends such as Automatic1111 save the prompt, negative prompt, seed, sampler and model name in a PNG text chunk called parameters. ComfyUI saves the whole node graph in prompt and workflow chunks. Anyone who downloads the PNG can read exactly how you made it.

A note on honesty: removing metadata is not the same as hiding that an image is AI-generated. Some generators also embed an invisible watermark in the pixels themselves (Google's SynthID is one example). Metadata X-ray does not touch pixels, so pixel watermarks are left exactly as they were. And on many platforms, labelling AI content is a rule you are expected to follow.

Lossless removal: why image quality doesn't change

Many metadata removers work by decoding the image and saving it again. That drops the metadata, but it also re-compresses the picture, so every pass costs a little quality, and JPEGs pick up new artifacts.

Metadata X-ray works at the file-structure level instead. It reads the file as a list of blocks: JPEG markers, PNG chunks or WebP chunks. It then writes a new file that keeps the blocks holding pixel data and drops the blocks holding metadata. The compressed image data is copied byte for byte. After cleaning, the tool reads its own output again and compares the image data with the original. The "Verified" message comes from that comparison; it isn't assumed.

Two blocks are kept by default because removing them changes how the photo looks:

  • Color profile (ICC). Tells viewers which color space the image uses. Without it, photos from wide-gamut phones can look dull or oversaturated. A color profile describes your device's display or camera model at most, not you.
  • Rotation. Phones usually store portrait photos sideways and add a flag saying "rotate this". Removing all EXIF would remove that flag, and the photo would show up sideways. Metadata X-ray writes back a tiny EXIF block containing only the rotation value.

Untick either option to remove them too.

How to use it

  1. Drop one or more photos onto the box above, paste one, or click to choose files.

Questions

Don't social networks remove metadata anyway?

Several large platforms remove metadata from the copy they show to other people. But they still receive your original upload, with everything in it. And many other ways of sharing keep the file exactly as it is: email attachments, files sent "as a document" in messaging apps, cloud-storage share links, forums and marketplaces. Cleaning the file before it leaves your device is the only way to be sure.

Can removed metadata be recovered?

Not from the cleaned file: the bytes are gone, not hidden. Your original file still has everything, so keep or delete it as you see fit.

Does it remove watermarks?

No. Visible watermarks and invisible pixel watermarks are part of the image data, which this tool deliberately never changes.

Why does my photo have no GPS data?

Location tagging may be turned off in your camera settings, or the app you used to export the photo may have removed it already. It's still worth checking for serial numbers, thumbnails and timestamps.

Is my photo uploaded anywhere?

No. The file is read and rewritten inside this browser tab. The network meter at the top of the page counts every byte the page sends, and the page's security policy forbids connections to any other site.
