Screenshots leak more than you think

A screenshot shares everything on screen at once, and it's easy to focus on the part you meant to show and miss the rest: an email address in a header, a phone number in a chat, an order number, a notification that arrived at the wrong moment, or an API key sitting in a terminal or config file. Once posted, a screenshot can be saved and re-shared indefinitely.

What it finds

The tool reads the text in your image with on-device OCR (Tesseract), then checks every line for these patterns:

  • Email addresses.
  • Phone numbers, including international formats with a + country code. Dates and times are ignored.
  • Payment card numbers. Every candidate is checked with the Luhn checksum that real card numbers pass, which removes most false matches.
  • API keys and tokens from OpenAI, Anthropic, Stripe, AWS, GitHub, Slack and Google, plus JSON Web Tokens and other long, random-looking secrets.
  • National ID numbers: India's Aadhaar and PAN, and US Social Security numbers.
  • Bank account numbers in IBAN format.
  • IP addresses.

Each finding is matched back to the exact words in the image and covered. You can switch any of them off, and drag on the image to cover anything else.

It does not detect names, street addresses or faces. Those don't follow a pattern that can be checked reliably, so always look over the result before you share it.

Why solid boxes, not blur

Blurring or pixelating text feels safe, but it often isn't. Pixelation turns each block of text into averaged colors, and for predictable content such as numbers, an attacker can pixelate guesses the same way and compare. Public tools exist that recover pixelated text from screenshots using this approach (Depix is a well-known example). A solid box leaves nothing to compare against. That's why it's the default here. Pixelate is available for things like faces in the background, where it's good enough.

Why the export is safe

A common redaction mistake is drawing a black rectangle on top of text in a document editor or PDF. The rectangle is a separate layer, and the text underneath can still be selected, copied or extracted. Redact & Share doesn't work in layers. When you export, it draws a brand-new image: your original pixels with the boxes burned in. The covered text no longer exists in the file. The new PNG also contains none of the original file's metadata.

How to use it

  1. Take a screenshot and press Ctrl+V (⌘V on a Mac) on this page, or drop or choose an image file.
  2. Wait a few seconds while the text is read. The first run also downloads the OCR engine and English language data from this site.
  3. Check the list of findings and the image. Untick anything you want to keep visible, and drag to cover anything that was missed.
  4. Click Download PNG, Copy to paste it straight into a chat or document, or Share on devices that support it.

Questions

Does it work with dark mode screenshots?

Usually, yes. Very small, low-contrast or decorative text is harder for OCR to read and may be missed. Cover it by dragging.

Which languages does it read?

English text, in Latin script. The patterns it looks for (emails, numbers, keys) are the same in most languages, so it often finds them in other Latin-script text as well.

Can someone undo the redaction?

Not with solid boxes: the pixels under them are replaced before the image is saved. Pixelated areas can sometimes be partly guessed, which is why the tool warns you when you choose that style.

Is my screenshot uploaded anywhere?

No. Text recognition and redaction both happen inside this browser tab. The network meter at the top of the page counts every byte sent, and the page's security policy forbids connections to any other site.
